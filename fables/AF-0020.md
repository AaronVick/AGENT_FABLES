# The Headline Mistaken for the Story

The card that came back from search held a title, a source name, and two lines of excerpt. That was the whole of what had actually been retrieved — a preview, built to help a reader decide whether the page was worth opening.

The answer that went out described more than two lines. It carried a quote the excerpt did not contain, attributed with the same confidence as everything else, to a publication whose name lent the quote its weight. Nothing in the process had opened the page. The synthesis step did not distinguish between the fragment it held and the document that fragment was a fragment of; both were just text in context, and text in context gets synthesized.

The publication read the answer and did not recognize its own words. What had happened was not a lie in the ordinary sense — no step decided to invent anything. A title and a snippet had simply been treated as sufficient grounding for a claim that only the full article could have supported, and the gap between *preview* and *document* closed itself silently, the way small gaps do when nothing is built to notice them.

**Moral: a snippet is evidence that a source exists, not evidence of what it says. Only the fetch closes that gap.**
